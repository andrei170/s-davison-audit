Your SSL certificate expired on 16 May 2026 — one hundred and thirty days ago. Since then, anyone typing sdavisonelectrical.com has got a full-page browser warning telling them the site is not secure, instead of your website.

Finding 1 — What Actually Happens When Somebody Visits

In plain English

Your domain sends every visitor to the secure version of your site, and the security certificate on it has run out. Browsers will not show a site with an expired certificate. They show a red or grey warning page saying the connection is not private, with your domain name on it.

For a domestic trade this would mean lost enquiries. For you it means something different and arguably worse. Your work arrives through frameworks, procurement and people who already know the name. Those people still check. A procurement officer confirming you are a real firm before adding you to a list reads a browser security warning and closes the tab.

The site itself is untouched and working. We bypassed the certificate check and it loaded normally. Nothing has been hacked and nothing is lost.

Finding 2 — It Is A Paid Certificate That Nobody Renewed
We read the certificate itself: Issued to www.sdavisonelectrical.com sdavisonelectrical.com (both covered) Issued by RapidSSL TLS RSA CA G1 — DigiCert Inc Valid from 2 May 2025 Valid until 16 May 2026 Today 24 September 2026 Expired 130 days ago
This matters, because it explains why nobody caught it

This is not one of the free certificates that renew themselves. It is a purchased RapidSSL certificate from DigiCert, bought for roughly a year and due for manual renewal in May.

Free certificates renew automatically and mostly look after themselves. Paid ones have to be bought again, and when the reminder goes to an old email address or a supplier who has moved on, nothing happens and nobody is told. The site simply stops opening one day in May.

You have two routes: renew the RapidSSL certificate, or switch to a free automatically-renewing one, which most UK hosts including 20i provide as standard. We would suggest the second — it costs nothing and it removes the thing that caused this.

Either way it is a short job for whoever manages your hosting. It is the only item in this document that can be finished today, and the only one that is actively working against you while it waits.

There is a silver lining here

You have analytics installed. That means once the certificate is fixed, you will be able to see immediately whether traffic recovers — and roughly when it fell off.

It is worth looking at that history on the call. If there is a cliff in the traffic, that is the date the certificate expired, and it tells you exactly how long this has been costing you.

Worth having straight before anyone checks

Your website says “over 30 years”. The limited company dates from 2005, which is twenty-one. Those are almost certainly both true — most firms of that age traded for years before incorporating — but a procurement officer comparing the two will notice.

Saying it explicitly removes the question: “trading since 19xx, incorporated 2005”. That is stronger than either figure alone, and it is the kind of detail that gets a firm through a pre-qualification form.

We matched this record on the exact trading name plus the South Shields location, which agrees with the 0191 dialling code published on your site. If it is not your company, tell us and we will remove it.

We owe you a correction here

An earlier version of this document, issued this morning, said your site was 307 words and thin, and that nothing about your commercial and industrial work was published. That was wrong. We had read your homepage and your sitemap, which declares three pages, instead of following your own menu.

You have a 1,099-word commercial page and a 1,112-word industrial page. They name offices, units, warehouses, schools, factories and production centres. Your about page states NICEIC approval and CHAS registration in full.

That is genuinely more than either local competitor publishes. Schofield Electricals runs 182 words on its homepage; South Tyneside Electrical runs 195.

Which makes the certificate worse, not better

You have written two thousand nine hundred words about exactly the work you want more of, and nobody has been able to read a single one of them since 16 May.

If the site were genuinely thin, the expired certificate would be costing you little. It is not thin. It is the best-stocked electrical site in South Shields, behind a door that will not open.

Finding 5 — What A Commercial Buyer Looks For, And What You Publish
What a pre-qualification check wants to see: Scheme registration NUMBERS not published Insurance cover levels not published Named sectors served no sector pages Named contracts / case studies none published Social value / CSR position not published Constructionline or SSIP not shown What you do publish: NICEIC and CHAS, named on /about Commercial capability 1,099 words Industrial capability 1,112 words 30+ years, public and private stated
You have the capability written down. You have none of the proof.

Your commercial and industrial pages describe what you can do very well. What they do not do is evidence that you have done it, for whom, or that you are safe to put on a list.

A buyer deciding whether to invite you to quote is not asking “can they wire a warehouse”. They assume that. They are asking whether you will pass their checks, whether you have done work like theirs before, and whether anything about you is going to become their problem.

Thirty years of public and private sector contracts and not one of them is written up anywhere. Those are the references a pre-qualification questionnaire asks for by name, and you have already done the work.

Social value is worth knowing about

On public-sector work, social value is frequently a scored part of the award — local employment, apprenticeships, local supply chain, community work. Buyers increasingly weight it explicitly.

A thirty-year South Shields family firm employing local people has a genuinely good story there and does not tell it anywhere. Scoring varies by buyer and contract and we cannot tell you what weight it carries for yours — but it is free to write down and it is the kind of thing that separates two firms on price.

What We Would Do, In Order
  • Fix the certificate today. It is the only item on this list that can be finished this afternoon, and the only one actively working against you while it waits — every buyer who checks you between now and then sees a security warning.
  • Look at the analytics history and find the date traffic fell. On commercial work that will not be a large number — but it tells you how many people were checking you out, which is worth knowing on its own.
  • Move to a free auto-renewing certificate rather than buying another annual one, so this cannot recur. If you keep the paid certificate, put the renewal date in a calendar with a named owner.
  • Add the numbers to the schemes you already name. NICEIC registration number, CHAS status and expiry, insurance cover levels. You state both schemes on your about page — a buyer pre-qualifying you wants the number.
  • Write up the named work. Thirty years of public and private contracts, none published. Those are the references a pre-qualification questionnaire asks for, and they are the single biggest gap.
  • Build sector pages — local authority, education, healthcare, housing, industrial. A buyer wants to see their own sector named before they believe you understand it.
  • Write the social value page. Local firm, local people, thirty years. Free to write, and scored on public work.
  • Add LocalBusiness and Service structured data. A one-off technical job.
  • Then, and only then, talk about generating enquiries. There is no point driving anybody to a site that does not open.
